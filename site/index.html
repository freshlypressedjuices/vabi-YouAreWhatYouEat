The world is changing what it eats

As countries get richer, diets move away from traditional, plant-heavy meals towards more sugar, processed meat and refined food. Researchers call this the nutrition transition (Popkin, 1993).

The shift is real

Diet-related disease is rising with it: across our countries, average adult obesity roughly doubled from 1990 to 2018, and diabetes rose from 7.8% to 13.1%.

But it gets little attention

Public debate about diet is mostly about trends like keto, vegan or intermittent fasting, not the slower, much larger change in what whole populations eat.

And the data is scattered

WHO's health statistics sit apart from diet data, and existing diet charts are separate and static. We bring diet, WHO targets and health together in one interactive tool.

Our data and how we prepared it

47 foods

Global Dietary Database

Tufts University's modelled estimates of average daily intake for 185 countries in 1990, 1995, 2000, 2005, 2010, 2015 and 2018.

8 targets

WHO healthy diet targets

Minimums such as at least 400 g of fruit and vegetables a day, and limits such as under 5 g of salt and under 10% of calories from free sugars.

3 outcomes

WHO Global Health Observatory

Adult obesity, adult diabetes, and the risk of dying early (ages 30 to 69) from non-communicable disease.

  1. Filtered over a million rows per food down to the all-population estimate for each country and year.
  2. Combined all 47 foods into one table, so one dropdown switches between them.
  3. Matched countries across datasets by ISO code and tagged each with its WHO region.
  4. Converted intake into WHO units and paired each country's diet with its health 5 or 10 years later.

We chose the Global Dietary Database over FAO food balance sheets because FAO measures food supply, not what people actually eat.

The guideline gap: no country meets all 8 WHO targets

Each country's diet is checked against WHO's eight healthy diet targets, every year. Change the year to see how compliance has moved since 1990.

Loading the interactive dashboard…

How we score it: a country meets a target if its intake is above the minimum or below the limit, giving a score out of 8. The gap % shows how far it is from each target, as a percentage of the target.

Diet and health: real links, but wealth muddies them

Choose a food, a health outcome, a 5 or 10-year lag and a diet year to see whether countries that ate more of something had different health later.

Loading the interactive dashboard…

These are Spearman correlations: countries are ranked on diet and on health, and we check whether the rankings move together, from −1 to +1. Ranking stops a few extreme countries from skewing the result.

What we found

Diets are converging

Sugar and processed foods are rising fastest where intake started low, in South-East Asia and Africa.

No country meets all 8 targets

Fruit and vegetables is the hardest target in every region, met by only 8.6% of countries.

Diet and health are linked, but not simply

Added sugar tracks with obesity, but wealth shapes both diet and health. These are associations, not causes.

Major challenges

  • FAO data measured supply, so we switched to GDD intake estimates.
  • GDD and WHO used different units, so we built conversions.
  • A few extreme countries flattened the map, so we used stepped colour bands.

Limitations

  • No income control: we can't separate diet from wealth.
  • Averages aren't population-weighted: a small island counts as much as India.
  • Some WHO targets are approximated, e.g. added sugars for free sugars.

Future work

  • Add GDP to separate diet from wealth.
  • Use population-weighted averages.
  • Show uncertainty ranges and add a country profile dashboard.
